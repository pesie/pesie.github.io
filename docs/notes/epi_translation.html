We Solve the Same Problems. We Just Use Different Words.

notes
methods
Published

June 2, 2026

I’ve spent most of my career trying to answer some version of the same question: why do outcomes differ across people, and can we actually trust the data telling us that they do?

That’s the center of epidemiology. But it also shows up constantly in product, strategy, evaluation, analytics, and basically anywhere decisions depend on drawing reliable conclusions from messy data.

A lot of those questions are ones epidemiologists are trained to answer. We just use different words for them.

Here are a few examples.

Practical question Epidemiologic translation
Did the campaign work? Did the exposure cause the outcome?
These two things move together. Does one cause the other? Is the association confounded?
Why does this work better for some groups? Is there effect modification?
Can we trust this metric? Is this measure valid?
Are the people giving us feedback representative? Is there selection bias?
Who is likely to churn, convert, or become high-cost? Who is at risk?
How much does context matter? Does the unit someone belongs to shape their outcome?
What meaningful groups exist? Are there latent subpopulations?

Did the campaign work?

Before giving credit to an intervention, you have to rule out everything else that changed at the same time. Sales went up, but was that the campaign? Or was it seasonality, or a competitor who exited the market?

An epidemiologist would ask, what would have happened if the intervention never occurred? Depending on the data structure, that might mean a difference-in-differences design or an interrupted time series.

These two things move together. Does one cause the other?

Association is often the start of the analysis, not the conclusion.

Customers who use a feature may spend more, but they may also have been more engaged before they ever used it. People who exercise more may also have higher incomes, better access to healthcare, and healthier diets.

A third factor can make two things appear connected even when one is not driving the other. Regression adjustment handles the cases where you can measure the confounders. Propensity score methods reconstruct comparability by balancing groups on observed characteristics before estimating the effect. When you can randomize, RCTs sidestep the problem entirely.

Why does this work better for some groups than others?

An average effect can hide everything interesting. The same product, campaign, or policy can work differently depending on who is receiving it, and sometimes that variation is the finding.

Knowing who it works for is often more useful than knowing whether it works on average. That’s what interaction terms and stratified analyses are for.

Can we trust this metric?

Before acting on a measure, it’s worth asking how it was constructed, what it captures, and what it might be excluding.

Construct validity asks whether the measure actually captures the underlying concept it’s supposed to represent. Reliability asks whether it does so consistently, across time, across raters, contexts. Both are questions you can ask of a customer satisfaction score or a disease surveillance system. The answers are not always reassuring.

Are the people giving us feedback representative?

The people in your data are not necessarily representative of the people you care about.

People who respond to a survey differ from people who don’t. Early adopters differ from later users. People who seek out a service differ from those who never enter the system. A big source of bias is often who we’re missing, and correcting for it usually means weighting or explicitly modeling who got left out.

Who is most likely to churn, convert, or become high-cost?

Many decisions depend on identifying who is most likely to experience an event before it happens and understanding what predicts that transition.

The outcome is different depending on the industry, but the problems and how we solve them are the same. Regression models can tell you who is at elevated risk at a point in time. When timing of the outcome matters, that means survival analysis. When the exposure or outcome is measured repeatedly, longitudinal modeling accounts for the fact that observations on the same person are not independent.

How much does context matter?

Individual outcomes don’t happen in a vacuum. Patients sit within provider networks. Members sit within health plans. Employees sit within teams.

When data has that kind of nested structure, standard models treat every observation as independent, which understates how much the context someone is in shapes their outcome. Multilevel models separate out how much of the variation is attributable to the individual versus the unit they belong to. That’s useful both for accurate estimation and for knowing where to intervene.

What meaningful groups exist?

People don’t behave as one homogeneous group, and they don’t always sort neatly into the categories we’d expect.

Sometimes the most useful insight comes from identifying groups of people who share patterns of behavior, attitudes, or needs, especially when those groups don’t map onto obvious demographic categories. Latent class analysis and clustering methods are built for exactly that.

Epidemiology is technically the study of disease distribution and determinants in populations. But in practice, it’s a set of tools for figuring out why outcomes differ and whether what you’re seeing is real.

The vocabulary is different. The underlying logic isn’t.
